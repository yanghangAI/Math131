Definition

A tangent line at a point is the straight line that just grazes the graph there, heading in the same direction the graph is heading at that point. (It may cross the graph somewhere else; what matters is the direction at that one point.) Its slope is the instantaneous rate of change — the speed at that exact moment.

But how can you find a line from a single point? You can't directly. The trick: take a secant through your point and a second point a distance away, then slide the second point in. As shrinks, the secants pivot toward one particular line — that limiting line is the tangent.

Definition

The vertical line is a vertical asymptote of if the outputs blow up as approaches from at least one side: or . On the graph, the curve hugs the line and races off the top or bottom of the page.

When does a fraction do this? When the bottom heads to 0 while the top heads to something nonzero. (Top and bottom are the numerator and denominator; we'll keep saying top and bottom.) Dividing a fixed-size number by a tinier and tinier number gives a bigger and bigger result: , ,

Definition

A hole is a single missing point on an otherwise unbroken curve — the open circle in the limit demo. It appears when the same factor is zero on the top and the bottom: the fraction is there, undefined, but once that factor is cancelled the simplified formula gives a perfectly ordinary height and the graph is calm. A hole is not an asymptote: nothing blows up. (If the bottom is still zero after cancelling — say at — then it is an asymptote after all.)

The procedure: find the asymptotes, then sketch

“Confirm with a graph” doesn't need a calculator. Every rational function (a polynomial over a polynomial, like all three here) can be sketched from its factored form, and the first two steps are exactly the ones that find the asymptotes.

  1. Factor and cancel. Write the top and bottom in factors and cancel any factor they share. Note the where a cancelled factor was zero — that's a hole.
  2. Asymptotes and holes. Draw a dashed vertical line at each zero of the surviving bottom: those are the vertical asymptotes. Mark each hole with an open circle; its height is the simplified formula evaluated at the cancelled .
  3. Signs. Cut the -axis at every asymptote and at every zero of the simplified top. On each piece the function keeps one sign — it can only switch by passing through zero (top ) or by jumping across a break (bottom ), and you have cut at all of those places — so one easy test number per piece reads the sign off. Any number strictly inside the piece will do; if it's available. Positive beside an asymptote means the curve shoots up there; negative means down.
  4. Far away. Plug in and to see what height the two tails are approaching.
  5. Connect. In each piece, one smooth stroke through the test point that obeys the signs and never crosses a dashed line.
